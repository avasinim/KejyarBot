## Lesson 36 — complements and sentence constituents
1. The lesson correctly rejects the overly broad definition «everything except Subject and Verb is a complement». Object, noun complement, noun modifier and adjunct/adverbial are distinct analytical categories.
2. Distinguish complement in the broad syntactic sense from Object as a grammatical function; do not use «بەرکار» and «تەواوکەر» as synonyms.
3. The examples «گەڵای دارەکە» and «شووشەی پەنجەرەکە» require a consistent NP-internal analysis: head noun, ezafe linker, dependent/complement or modifier. Avoid presenting possession as the only possible relation; part–whole and classificatory relations are also possible.
4. «زیرەک» in «منداڵی زیرەک» is an NP-internal modifier, not a verbal complement. Keep word class (adjective) separate from syntactic function (modifier).
5. The deletion test for adjuncts is only a diagnostic, not a decisive rule. Predicate-selected location or other complements can look optional in isolated examples; check lexical valency and contextual interpretation.
6. «لە ئاودا» with «دەژی» and «هەورامان» in «گوێزی هەورامان» need contextual/source checking before assigning a fixed function. Retain the draft's own uncertainty.
7. The source draft contains duplicated cross-reference sections and non-sequential section numbering. Record as editorial cleanup items; do not delete original text during the audit.

## Lesson 37 — sentence types by structure
1. The clause (پێڕستە) is the relevant unit for distinguishing simple, compound, complex and compound-complex sentences; counting finite verbs alone is not sufficient.
2. Keep sentence (ڕستە) distinct from clause (پێڕستە), and independent clause distinct from subordinate clause. An overt subject is not obligatory in every clause.
3. A simple sentence may contain coordinated NPs or multiple modifiers and still have one independent clause. Coordination of phrases is not automatically coordination of clauses.
4. A compound sentence requires two or more independent clauses; a complex sentence requires a main clause and at least one subordinate clause; compound-complex requires at least two independent clauses and at least one subordinate clause.
5. Distinguish coordinating conjunctions («و، بەڵام، یان، نە…نە») from subordinators («چونکە، ئەگەر، هەرچەندە، کاتێک») and discourse connectives/adverbs («بۆیە، کەوایە، هێشتا»). These are not one uniform class.
6. «کە» is multifunctional: its category/function depends on the construction. Do not classify every occurrence as a subordinating conjunction.
7. Examples with ellipsis, shared subjects, or paired «یان» need clause-boundary and parallelism annotation. Poetry examples must retain source attribution and should not be used as sole evidence for ordinary prose syntax.
8. The draft's Course 1 identity and runtime=false status remain binding.

## Lesson 38 — clause, main clause and subordinate clause
1. Preserve the distinction: a sentence is a complete unit of utterance; a clause is a grammatical constituent within a sentence. A sentence may contain one or multiple clauses.
2. A main clause is structurally independent in the relevant construction; a subordinate clause depends syntactically or semantically on another clause. Avoid defining every subordinate clause solely as «incomplete meaning»—some are pragmatically interpretable in isolation but syntactically dependent.
3. The list of subordinating markers is construction-sensitive. «کە»، «کو» and «ئەوەی» can have different functions; classify each occurrence by its syntactic distribution and role.
4. «مادام» can mark condition, duration or continuity according to context; do not assign a single invariant relation.
5. Distinguish clause type (relative, complement, adverbial) from semantic relation (cause, condition, concession, time). A marker's meaning alone does not fully determine clause category.
6. Punctuation placement depends on clause order and writing convention; it is not a diagnostic of subordination itself.
7. Retain pedagogical additions as such; distinguish them from source-segment examples and dialect-attested evidence.

## Lesson 39 — object and its grammatical functions
1. Keep Object (بەرکار) as a grammatical function distinct from Agent (ئەنجامدەر), Subject (بکەر/نیهاد) and Recipient/Goal (وەرگر/ئاراستە).
2. The examples «کتێبەکەی خوێندەوە» and «من تۆم بینی» support direct-object identification; physical affectedness is not a necessary semantic property of an object.
3. In «من کتێبێکم بە هاوڕێکەم دا», «کتێبێک» is the direct object and «بە هاوڕێکەم» is a recipient/goal phrase in the draft's descriptive terminology. Whether to call it an indirect object is framework-dependent; preserve the pending terminology decision.
4. A prepositional phrase is not automatically an indirect object. «لە قوتابییەکە پرسی» needs verb-specific valency and framework analysis.
5. Object realization may be a noun, pronoun or full NP; identify the complete constituent, not only the head noun.
6. Not every clause has a direct object. Keep transitive/intransitive behavior separate from Subject/Agent roles and from the presence of oblique phrases.
7. The draft's contrast among Object, Complement and Adjunct must be reconciled with Lesson 36 through one explicit framework and terminology register.

## Lesson 40 — transitive and intransitive verbs
1. Define transitivity in terms of a verb's syntactic valency and its use with a direct object, not physical impact or a simplistic «what?» test alone.
2. Distinguish lexical valency/potential from the surface realization of an object in a particular clause. An object can be omitted or understood from context; absence of an overt object does not by itself prove lexical intransitivity.
3. Keep object omission/object drop distinct from genuinely intransitive use. The distinction is context- and verb-specific and requires naturally attested Sorani examples.
4. Passive alternation can help diagnose transitivity, but it is not an exceptionless test. Not every transitive construction passivizes identically, and passive formation is constrained by semantics, dialect and construction.
5. A verb may show transitive/intransitive alternation with a change in argument structure or meaning (e.g. the draft's break/open examples). Analyze each construction, not just the dictionary lemma.
6. Compound status and transitivity are independent dimensions: compound verb formation does not entail transitivity.
7. Clitic forms such as «ـمان، ـتان، ـیان» are not a universal transitivity test; their function may be possessive, object, agent marking or agreement depending on construction and variety. Cross-reference Lessons 11–12.
8. The contrast «نان خوارد» requires a construction-level and lexical analysis; do not automatically assume «نان» is always an external direct object or always part of a lexical compound.

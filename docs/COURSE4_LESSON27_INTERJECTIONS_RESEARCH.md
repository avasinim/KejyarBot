# Course 4 — Lesson 27: Interjections and Exclamatives in Sorani Kurdish

## Scope
This lesson separates the lexical/interactional category **Interjection** from **Exclamation/Exclamative** at utterance or clause level, and from **Vocative**. The owner's proposed forms are retained as candidate data, not treated as a validated closed inventory.

## Core sources
1. Ameka, Felix K. (1992). “Interjections: The Universal Yet Neglected Part of Speech.” *Journal of Pragmatics* 18(2–3): 101–118. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/0378-2166(92)90060-G. See especially pp. 113–115 for expressive, conative and phatic functions and the distinction between interjections, particles and vocatives.
2. McCarus, Ernest N. (2009). “Kurdish.” In Gernot Windfuhr (ed.), *The Iranian Languages*, pp. 587–633. See pp. 614–615. The chapter focuses on Sulaimania Kurdish and lists introductory expressions such as *ay, hā, xō, āferīn, amān,* and *ōxay*, while showing that some forms also perform topic-management or response functions.
3. Taha, Tola Najm (2022). *The Functions of Intonation in English and Central Kurdish: A Phonological Comparative Study*. MA thesis, Charmo University. See pp. 77–79 (thesis pagination): Central Kurdish exclamatives, exclamative words and intonation. The thesis lists forms transliterated as *ax, of, wai, her bi ji, aferin, oxei, pekw,* and *ai*.
4. Fossum, L. O. (1919). *A Practical Kurdish Grammar*, Chapter IX, pp. 214–215. This historical source lists a range of expressions including *ai, āyi, ai-hō, ākh, wāi, hāwār, amān, dāi,* and *āferīn*. It is not used as a definitive description of contemporary standard Sorani because its dialect scope and orthographic conventions are historically situated.

## Analytical decisions
- **Interjection** is treated as a word/form class that can constitute a relatively independent utterance.
- **Exclamation/Exclamative** is treated as an utterance/clause-level phenomenon. An exclamative can be formed without a standalone interjection, including through intonation and clause structure.
- **Vocative** addresses an addressee (often a name or noun phrase). Arabic *ḥarf nidāʾ* is therefore closer to *Vocative particle*, not a general equivalent of *Interjection*.
- Ameka's three functional groupings are used: expressive (speaker state), conative (directed at addressee), and phatic (contact/interaction management). These are functional groupings, not an exhaustive Sorani word list.
- Interjections and discourse particles are not collapsed. The same form may have multiple uses; classification must rely on syntax, pause/prosody, conversational position and context.

- Several other forms in the inventory require speaker-verified examples, region information and prosodic recordings before assigning a stable category or meaning.

## Evidence limitations
The sources differ in scope and method: McCarus focuses on Sulaimania Kurdish; Taha's thesis discusses Central Kurdish exclamatives and intonation; Fossum is a historical grammar. None of these sources validates every item in the owner's list across all Sorani varieties. The lesson therefore avoids universal claims and marks uncertain forms for later corpus/speaker validation.
